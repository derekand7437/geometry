Unit 01

Angles & parallel lines

One transversal across two parallel lines makes eight angles — but only two different sizes. Learn which name goes with which pair and the rest is arithmetic.

What to remember

  • Only two sizes exist. Angles 1, 4, 5, 8 are all congruent, and so are 2, 3, 6, 7. Any angle from one group plus any angle from the other adds to 180°.
  • Vertical angles are across the X from each other and always congruent — this one does not need parallel lines.
  • Corresponding angles sit in matching corners at the two intersections. Congruent when the lines are parallel.
  • Alternate interior angles are between the parallel lines, on opposite sides of the transversal. Congruent.
  • Same-side interior angles are between the lines on the same side. Supplementary — they add to 180°.
  • A linear pair is any two angles that make a straight line together. Always supplementary, parallel or not.
congruent: vertical, corresponding, alternate  |  supplementary: linear pair, same-side
Unit 02

Triangle congruence

Five shortcuts prove two triangles identical without checking all six parts — and two famous non-shortcuts that prove nothing.

What to remember

  • SSS — three pairs of sides match. Rigid: a triangle with fixed sides has only one shape.
  • SAS — two sides and the angle between them. The angle must be included, sandwiched by the two sides.
  • ASA — two angles and the side between them.
  • AAS — two angles and a side outside them. Legal, because knowing two angles gives you the third.
  • HL — right triangles only: hypotenuse plus one leg.
  • SSA and AAA prove nothing. SSA can be swung into two different triangles; AAA gives a similar shape at any size.
  • Reflexive side or angle: when two triangles share a segment, that segment is congruent to itself — it is often the third fact you need.

The Pythagorean theorem plus two triangles whose sides you should know by heart, because they appear on every test without measurements.

  • a² + b² = c², and c is always the hypotenuse — the side across from the right angle, never a leg.

Unit 04

Similarity

Same shape, different size. Angles are untouched; every length is multiplied by one number, and area is multiplied by its square.

What to remember

  • Similar means all corresponding angles are congruent and all corresponding sides are proportional.
  • AA is enough. Two pairs of congruent angles prove similarity — you never need a side.
  • Scale factor k = new length ÷ old length. Set up a proportion with matching sides and cross-multiply.
  • Perimeter scales by k, area scales by k², volume by k³. Double the sides and the area quadruples.
  • Write the similarity statement in order. ▵ABC ~ ▵DEF means A pairs with D, so AB pairs with DE.
  • A line parallel to one side of a triangle cuts the other two sides proportionally, which is where most in-class problems come from.
AB/DE = BC/EF = AC/DF = k  |  area ratio = k²
Unit 05

Circles

Every circle rule is really about one number — the arc. Where the angle's vertex sits decides whether you get the arc, half of it, or something in between.

What to remember

  • A central angle equals its arc. Vertex at the center, so a 70° angle cuts a 70° arc.
  • An inscribed angle is half its arc. Vertex on the circle. The same arc gives every inscribed angle the same measure.
  • An angle inscribed in a semicircle is 90° — the arc is 180°, so the angle is half of that.
  • A tangent meets the radius at 90° at the point of contact, which turns most tangent problems into Pythagorean problems.
  • Arc length = (θ/360) × 2πr. Sector area = (θ/360) × πr². Both are just a fraction of the whole circle.
  • The whole way around is 360°, so a major arc is 360 minus its minor arc.
C = 2πr  |  A = πr²  |  inscribed = arc ÷ 2
Unit 06

Coordinate geometry

Geometry on a grid, where three formulas answer almost everything — and two of them are secretly the Pythagorean theorem.

What to remember

  • Distance = √((x₂−x₁)² + (y₂−y₁)²). It is the Pythagorean theorem with the legs measured off the grid.
  • Midpoint = ((x₁+x₂)/2, (y₁+y₂)/2). Average the x's, average the y's — the answer is a point, not a number.
  • Slope = (y₂−y₁)/(x₂−x₁), rise over run. Keep the points in the same order top and bottom.
  • Parallel lines have equal slopes. Perpendicular slopes are opposite reciprocals: 2/3 becomes −3/2, and their product is −1.
  • Proving a shape on a grid means picking the right tool: distance for side lengths, slope for right angles and parallel sides.
  • Horizontal lines have slope 0; vertical lines have undefined slope, because you would be dividing by zero.
d = √(Δx² + Δy²)  |  M = (x̄, ȳ)  |  m = Δy/Δx
